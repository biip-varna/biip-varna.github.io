Bulgaria's Energy Security Needs a Financial Test

By Gustavo Pessoa

An electricity interconnector is usually discussed as a line on a map: a physical link that allows power to cross a border. For a factory deciding whether to invest, however, the more important question is what that connection means for the cost and reliability of electricity over time. A new link can create options without making every price predictable. Energy security begins with infrastructure, but it is tested through contracts, finance and the ability to use that infrastructure when conditions change.

Bulgaria's position in South-East Europe makes this distinction important. The European Commission's CESEC initiative brings together governments and partners to improve cross-border connections and integrate energy markets. A Commission analysis of the Bulgaria–Greece electricity relationship describes a striking change: Bulgaria has increasingly imported electricity during daylight hours and exported it in the evening, a pattern associated with renewable generation, storage and interconnected markets. These are observations about market flows, not a promise that prices will always be lower or that every hour will follow the same pattern.

The change suggests a practical question for Bulgarian policymakers. Instead of asking only how much electricity can cross a border, they should also ask which households and businesses can benefit from the new options, under what terms, and who bears the cost when forecasts prove wrong.

A small food processor, for example, may be able to shift some refrigeration or production to hours when power is cheaper. Doing so may require a control system, backup equipment or a contract with a supplier. The investment is not justified merely because an interconnector exists. It makes sense if savings, reliability and financing costs together support it. This is an illustrative case, not a claim about a named company or a particular Bulgarian tariff.

Large firms can often negotiate electricity contracts and manage fluctuations across several activities. Smaller firms may face less room to absorb a sudden increase or to finance an investment whose benefit arrives gradually. Banks should therefore assess energy-saving or storage investments against a range of plausible prices and usage patterns, rather than an optimistic single projection. Borrowers, in turn, need to understand whether a contract contains fixed prices, variable charges, penalties for early termination or obligations that change when demand differs from forecast.

Public support can help where a project produces benefits beyond the firm receiving the loan. It should not remove the discipline of asking whether that project works. A guarantee should identify the loss it covers, its duration and the risks that remain with the lender and borrower. A subsidy should disclose its cost and purpose. Otherwise, a policy designed to reduce vulnerability can move it quietly onto the public balance sheet.

Electricity security also depends on the details of market access. An interconnector can be available on paper while capacity is constrained at a particular hour or in a particular direction. Network maintenance and system reliability matter; so do the rules by which capacity is offered and the information available to users. European policy already recognises that interconnection projects require economic and environmental assessment. The question is not whether every proposal should be built, but whether the benefits and trade-offs are clear before commitments are made.

Storage creates another set of choices. A battery can move electricity from one hour to another, provide system services or help a user manage demand. These uses have different revenues and risks. A business financing a battery should not be told that income from all three is guaranteed. Policy should distinguish an existing revenue stream from one that depends on future market rules, and the life of the equipment from the term of the loan that pays for it.

The same care applies to regional cooperation. Bulgaria, Greece and their neighbours do not need identical energy systems to benefit from trade. They do need compatible information, credible operating rules and ways to handle disruptions. A joint exercise could examine what happens when generation falls, a line is unavailable or a price spike persists. Publishing the lessons, without exposing commercially sensitive details, would let businesses and citizens see which risks were tested and which remain.

There is a social dimension too. A lower average market price does not automatically produce a lower bill for every household. Network charges, the timing of consumption and the design of a contract influence the final amount paid. Any account of the transition should therefore separate the wholesale price from the bill customers actually receive, and assess the effects on people with the least ability to shift consumption or make an upfront investment.

The European debate often treats an interconnector as evidence of integration. It is evidence of possibility. The stronger test is whether the connection expands real choices without hiding new risks in loan agreements, electricity bills or public guarantees. For Bulgaria, a more connected grid can support a more secure economy. The financial terms are what determine how widely that security is shared.

Gustavo Pessoa is an economist and professor at Fundação Getulio Vargas, Brazil. His work addresses financial regulation, economic resilience and public policy.
