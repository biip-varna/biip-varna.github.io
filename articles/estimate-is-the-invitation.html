The Estimate Is the Invitation

On Thursday afternoon, while a government aircraft was still somewhere between Rotterdam and Belgrade, Aleksandar Vučić stood in front of cameras in Čoka and read out a number. The assessments of the Security Information Agency, the Military Security Agency, "and even the Military Intelligence Agency" (i čak i Vojno-obaveštajne agencije), and the Interior Ministry, Serbia's president said, ran to tens of thousands of people at the burial of Ratko Mladić. The state would provide "every kind of logistical and transport support" and preserve "public order, peace and security." Where and when the funeral would be held was for the family to say.

Read that as a security briefing and it is unremarkable. Read it as what it is, a head of state publishing the attendance figure for a genocide convict's funeral four days in advance, and it is the most important sentence spoken in Serbia this week.

Nobody who heard it concluded they should stay home. They concluded the state had counted them in. Vučić did not say come. He said the services expect you, and that they will keep you safe when you do. That is a mobilisation order with the letterhead of four agencies on it, military intelligence included, and it will never be quoted back to him as one, because the words are about security.

Presidents choose which intelligence estimates they announce on television.

A state holds thousands of assessments its citizens never hear. Consider the one selected for national broadcast. Not that violence was expected. Not that a threat had been detected. Not that anyone should keep away from a particular street on Monday. The single piece of intelligence chosen for the country was the size of the crowd at Ratko Mladić's funeral. Tens of thousands. The number tells the man watching in Niš that travelling to Belgrade will not put him on a fringe; he will be one of tens of thousands. It tells the veteran in Republika Srpska that he is not intruding on a private burial; Serbia's head of state has already described the crowd he will join. Nobody needs an invitation once the attendance has been announced.

It is the same grammar that has governed every hour since the death. The family decides; the state sends the aircraft. "General Mladić is a general and the protocols are known," the Serbian justice minister, Nenad Vujić, told Banja Luka television; the state supplies the military honours. The people are coming; the state supplies the estimate. Each act of authorship is presented as a response to something already under way. A week ago in Osečina, Vučić told journalists that everything they were reading about honours was untrue and that the state would help only to make the funeral dignified and decent. He was not lying. He does not give orders in a form that can be held against him. He describes what is about to happen and lets the description do the ordering.

What is about to happen has already had its first act. The coffin came off the plane on the shoulders of a Serbian Army detail, in camouflage and helmets, under the flags of Serbia and Republika Srpska. Vujić stood on the apron. Along the motorway to the Military Medical Academy, gendarmes saluted the van as it passed. People stood on the overpasses with banners reading that heroes never die. A pro-government tabloid carried the convoy live. None of this has been declared a state funeral. That is the point. The state is already performing one, without the declaration, and the second act is booked for Saturday noon in the Dom Vojske, the army's own hall, where military protocol commemorates its deceased officers. The third is Monday at Topčider, with buses from Republika Srpska arranged by the veterans' organisations in cooperation with the authorities on both sides of the Drina. When the crowd is the size Vučić forecast, he will say the state merely kept order. Brussels will be told logistics. The crowd will have been told something else, by the same sentence.

The picture on the loader carries more than a body. Two flags on one coffin, borne by the army of one state for the commander of the army of another, is the whole of Srpski Svet in a single frame, and what it tells everyone across the Drina and the Ibër (Ibar) is that Mladić, in Belgrade, is still a general. A country in the third year of the region's steepest rearmament has just shown its soldiers whose coffin they salute. That is a subject for another day. It is enough here to say that the funeral is not only being staged for the crowd at Topčider.

How does a candidate for membership of the European Union bury a convicted genocide commander? Serbia is answering in real time. The man in the coffin is the highest-ranking officer convicted of genocide on European soil since 1945, by a tribunal the Security Council created and whose judgments Serbia's predecessor state undertook, in Article 1(2) of its April 2002 cooperation law, to "respect and implement." The enlargement commissioner told the Bled forum on Monday that glorification of war criminals has no place in candidate countries, and that this is the basis on which Brussels will assess what Belgrade does next. She goes to Serbia on the ninth of September. The grave will be two days old.

She will find nothing to point at. That is the design. No decree, no declared day of mourning, no minister's signature on an order of honours. A family's wishes, a security assessment, a hall that happened to be available, an army detail that happened to be at the airport. Serbia's president has found a way to hold a state funeral without ever being present at the decision to hold one.

There is one thing she can point at, because he said it himself, on camera, before the body had landed. Tens of thousands. The number was a promise, not a warning. Everything the state has done since has been arranged to keep it.

He did not invite them. He counted them.
