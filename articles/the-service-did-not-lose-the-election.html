The Service Did Not Lose the Election

Russia’s foreign intelligence service defended Vučić against the students. It now defends the students against Brussels. Only one of the three has stayed where it was.

By Drizan Shala | 18 November 2026

On 15 September 2025 the press bureau of Russia’s Foreign Intelligence Service, the SVR, announced that the European Union was preparing a “Serbian Maidan”. The students filling Serbian streets were, in that text, a product of European subversion, brainwashed by EU-funded media, and the anniversary of the Novi Sad canopy collapse was the date chosen for the coup. Aleksandar Vučić, travelling in Japan, thanked the “Russian partners” for the information and ordered his services to ask them for more. The students answered the SVR in public within two days.

On 17 November 2026 the same press bureau wrote about the same students. They had won a parliamentary election three weeks earlier, and that morning their deputies took their seats in the National Assembly. In the new text they are the “firmest and most independent leaders” (наиболее твердых и независимых лидеров) Serbia has, and the plot runs the other way. Brussels, the SVR says, is determined to force them into obedience. Urgent instructions have gone to the Belgrade stations of Germany’s BND and France’s DGSE to identify those leaders, isolate them and split the movement. The tools listed are bribery and compromising material.

Fourteen months separate the two texts. The students moved from one side of the accusation to the other, and the service stayed with its interest. In 2025 it defended a government that gave Moscow three things: no recognition of Kosovo, protection for Republika Srpska, no sanctions on Russia. In 2026 it lists those three things by name and attaches them to the new majority as the reason Ursula von der Leyen’s office is irritated.

A service holds a veto over a political environment when it can say in public what a government may not do, and the government complies or keeps quiet. In March 2025 Vučić’s deputy prime minister, Aleksandar Vulin, said that Russian services were helping the government against the protests. In April the government asked the FSB to rule on whether Serbian police had used a sonic weapon on Serbian citizens, and cited the answer. In May and June the SVR accused Serbian arms factories of supplying Ukraine and called it a stab in Russia’s back. Vučić halted ammunition exports. In September came the “Maidan” text and the president’s thanks.

The election of 25 October was supposed to end this. European capitals congratulated the winners within hours. On 16 November Aleksandar Baucal spoke for the Student List at the Commission’s Enlargement Forum in Brussels and left to an ovation. Marta Kos received the list’s representatives and wrote: “We share the same goal of a democratic Serbia.” Brussels then turned that sentiment into policy and asked Pristina to give the new leadership in Belgrade the benefit of the doubt.

On 12 November Dejan Šoškić and Miodrag Krmar of the Student List met the Russian ambassador, Aleksandr Botsan-Kharchenko. They met American diplomats the same day, as a new majority does. Five days later one of the capitals that had received the list had its intelligence service tell the list, in public, who its enemies are. The Russian Foreign Ministry carried the statement on X and Telegram.

A movement with no party structure and a parliamentary group one day old has now been told that European stations are hunting for its weak members. A deputy who argues for alignment with EU foreign policy has been described in advance as the bought one. A corruption story about a list figure will arrive already labelled a European operation, and a split will have its author before it happens. Those who hold Moscow’s three positions are covered by the word “independent”. The man applauded in Brussels is covered by nothing.

The Student List’s programme rejects Kosovo’s independence and opposes its membership in international organisations. Jovo Bakić, who leads the list’s parliamentary group, ruled out sanctions on Russia in the first days after the vote. Pristina is being asked for patience towards a majority whose Kosovo policy is the old one, and whose limits on that policy were restated on its first day in parliament by Russian intelligence.

In 2025, as protesters, the students answered the SVR. In 2026, as the majority, they have not. As of 18 November no leader of the Student List has said that Serbia’s incoming government does not need a Russian service to describe its interests, name its enemies or sort its members.

An unanswered statement is no proof of command, and no public document shows the Student List taking instructions from Moscow. What is public is a Russian service that drew the limits of Serbian policy under the old government, saw them obeyed, and drew them again for the new one on its first day, without a word of objection from those it addressed. Serbia’s voters removed a government on 25 October. The service that set its limits was not on the ballot.
